\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Root cause of the Pearson $r$ gap}
\label{sec:disc_precision}

Our reproduction recovers $r = 0.928$ between $f^+_{cp}$ and $H_p$,
falling $0.042$ below the paper's target of $r \approx 0.97$.
We attribute this gap to a single source: the floating-point precision
of pLDDT values in AlphaFold-DB v4.

In the paper's dataset --- most likely an earlier AlphaFold-DB version
where pLDDT appears to have been stored or rounded to integer values ---
a typical 300-residue protein has $\lesssim\!20$ distinct pLDDT levels,
each populated by many residues forming a substantial horizontal plateau
in $N_{cc}(\mathrm{pLDDT})$.
In AlphaFold-DB v4, the same protein can carry over 100 distinct
floating-point levels, so each level is typically visited by only 1--3
residues.
This shatters the plateaus that make peaks persistent.

The effect on $H_p$ is additive: more distinct persistence values in
$\mathcal{P}_D$ spread the probability mass function more evenly,
increasing $H_p$ by a roughly constant offset ($+0.12$ to $+0.16$ across
our three prototypes).
Both $f^+_{cp}$ and $H_p$ are shifted upward by similar amounts, so
the cloud of points in Figure~\ref{fig:scatter_corr} translates
diagonally without rotating.
A diagonal translation preserves the slope but increases the residual
variance around the regression line, which is exactly what reduces $r$
from $0.97$ to $0.928$.
This analysis predicts that applying a coarser discretiser (e.g.\
bin $\div 10$) would push $r$ closer to the paper's target while biasing
$H_p$ in the opposite direction; our diagnostic experiments confirmed
this trade-off (bin$\div10$ gives $r = 0.916$ but worsens the $H_p$
offset for all three prototypes).
We therefore retain \texttt{np.round} as the canonical discretiser.

\subsection{Methodological adaptations: the $n \geq 200$ and F1-only filters}
\label{sec:disc_filters}

Our Figure~7 analysis departs from the paper in two pre-processing choices
that require explicit justification, because both inflate our reported
Pearson $r$ relative to what a paper-faithful run on our data would give.

\paragraph{The F1-only restriction.}
We retain only first-fragment (F1) entries, reducing the dataset from
23{,}391 to 16{,}787 fragments.
The paper places no such restriction: Figure~8 of Cazals \& Sarti
explicitly includes an F4 entry (AF-P12111-F4-model\_v4), confirming that
all fragment files are treated as independent data points.
Our restriction was motivated by a concern that a protein split across F1,
F2, F3 contributes three correlated rows --- sharing the same organism,
domain architecture, and rough pLDDT regime --- which could artificially
tighten the scatter.
In practice this concern is overstated, because each fragment covers a
distinct peptide segment that may have entirely different pLDDT character
from its neighbours.
The F1-only filter therefore reduces the sample size by 28\% without a
corresponding theoretical grounding in the paper's framework.

\paragraph{The $n \geq 200$ filter on the correlation analysis.}
The paper uses the $n \geq 200$ criterion only as one leg of the
three-way fragmentation query in its Figure~8; the Figure~7 Pearson scatter
is computed over the full proteome.
We extend the filter to Figure~7 as well, because with floating-point
pLDDT a fragment of $n \lesssim 100$ residues can produce up to $n$
distinct persistence values.
At that granularity, $H_p$ grows with the count of distinct levels rather
than reflecting any biologically meaningful spread in domain confidences.
These short-fragment outliers form a dense cluster near
$(f^+_{cp} \approx 0.33,\; H_p \approx 0)$, adding residual variance
around the regression line.
Removing them raises $r$ from $0.807$ (all fragments, no filter) to
$0.928$ (F1 $+$ $n \geq 200$).
The paper does not encounter this artefact because its effectively
integer-valued pLDDT produces a coarse, informative persistence spectrum
even for short proteins.

\paragraph{Net effect of both filters.}
Both adaptations compensate for a single underlying cause --- float-precision
pLDDT, analysed in Section~\ref{sec:disc_precision} --- rather than
reproducing the paper's protocol.
A paper-faithful run on our data (all fragments, no length filter) would
recover $r \approx 0.807$; the 0.121 gap relative to our reported $r$,
and the 0.163 gap relative to the paper's target, are each attributable
to the same diagonal shift of the scatter cloud.

\subsection{Root cause of the PLM candidate gap}
\label{sec:disc_plm}

The 42-vs-86 discrepancy in fragmentation candidates follows from the
same mechanism, but acts on a different statistic.
PLM counts how many $N_{cc}$ peaks are \emph{persistent} at
$t_\nu = n \cdot t_p$, measured in connected-component-count units.
A peak requires a substantial horizontal plateau in $N_{cc}$ that
collapses slowly as pLDDT decreases.
With integer-valued pLDDT, 20--30 residues may share the same level,
creating a wide plateau where $N_{cc}$ stays elevated for a long
filtration interval; with float pLDDT, those residues are spread across
many individual levels, so the plateau disappears and the peak's
persistence drops below $t_\nu$.

This explains why the gap is multiplicative (${\approx}2\times$) rather
than additive: each domain's pLDDT peak either clears the persistence
threshold entirely or falls below it, with no partial credit.
Our $t_p = 0.020$ result (206 proteins) is the closest analogue to what
the paper sees at $t_p = 0.025$, suggesting that the effective threshold
in the paper's integer-pLDDT regime corresponds to a relative persistence
of about $0.020$ in our float-pLDDT regime.

The 42 proteins we do identify are the robustly fragmented cases whose
peaks survive even with the finer filtration granularity.
We expect this set to be a high-precision subset of the paper's 86,
containing the proteins with the most prominent and wide pLDDT
transitions between domains.

\subsection{Biological interpretation of the arity enrichment}
\label{sec:disc_arity}

The 3.08-fold enrichment of fragmented proteins in the arity bin
$(a_{25}=7, a_{75}=13)$ is statistically highly significant
($p = 1.76 \times 10^{-7}$) and biologically interpretable.
The arity range $[7, 13]$ at the 25th--75th percentile level corresponds
to proteins that are neither maximally disordered nor maximally compact:

\begin{itemize}
  \item A low-arity $a_{25} \approx 7$ means that a quarter of the
  protein's residues have fewer than 7 $\mathrm{C}_\alpha$ neighbours
  within 10\,\AA{}.
  This is characteristic of flexible linker regions with limited
  tertiary contacts.

  \item A moderate $a_{75} \approx 13$ means that three-quarters of
  residues have at most 13 neighbours, which is typical of well-packed
  globular domains but below the $a_{75} \approx 20$ seen in
  tightly-packed $\alpha/\beta$ proteins.
\end{itemize}

Together, this signature is exactly what one expects from a multi-domain
protein: compact, independently folded domains contribute the upper
quartile of arities, while disordered linkers contribute the lower
quartile.
Purely disordered proteins (like A0A0G2L439, $a_{75}=7$) have low arity
throughout, and single-domain globular proteins (like P15121, $a_{75}=23$)
have uniformly high arity.
Multi-domain fragmented proteins inhabit the intermediate zone where
packing heterogeneity is highest.

Crucially, this enrichment is derived from the 3D coordinate geometry
of the AlphaFold structure, entirely independently of the pLDDT
sequence analysis.
The fact that two orthogonal structural descriptors --- topological
pLDDT fragmentation and $\mathrm{C}_\alpha$ packing arity --- converge
on the same set of proteins provides mutual validation for both methods.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:disc_limits}

\paragraph{Single organism.}
All results are computed on the \textit{H.~sapiens} proteome only.
The paper demonstrates similar correlations across multiple model
organisms; whether the arity enrichment pattern generalises to other
proteomes remains untested.

\paragraph{No experimental disorder ground truth.}
We do not compare our fragmented candidates against DisProt or MobiDB
experimental annotations of intrinsic disorder.
Such a comparison would establish whether PLM$\,\geq 3$ proteins are
enriched in annotated IDRs or in multi-domain entries in the literature,
providing a biological validation beyond the internal cross-correlation.
This is out of scope for the current course project.

\paragraph{Data version.}
AlphaFold-DB v6 was released after this project began; all computations
use v4 PDB files.
The pLDDT distributions may differ between versions, and the arity
enrichment results could shift.
The float-precision gap that explains most of our discrepancies with
the paper may be reduced or exacerbated in v6.

\paragraph{Discretisation choice.}
The \texttt{np.round} discretiser is a pragmatic choice that minimises
the $H_p$ offset for the prototype proteins.
A principled derivation of the optimal discretiser from first principles
--- e.g., by matching the null-model $H_p$ distribution --- would
strengthen the theoretical grounding of our reproduction.

\subsection{Directions for future work}
\label{sec:disc_future}

The most direct extension is to apply the same pipeline to
AlphaFold-DB v6 and to additional model organisms
(\textit{M.\ musculus}, \textit{S.\ cerevisiae}) to assess the
universality of the arity enrichment signal.
A comparison of our 42 fragmented candidates against UniProt domain
annotations and known multi-domain proteins would provide external
biological validation.
On the algorithmic side, the batch-insertion filtration could be
generalised to other scalar functions on sequence-embedded graphs,
such as predicted solvent accessibility or AlphaFold's PAE matrix;
persistence of the resulting N-connected-component curves could yield
complementary fragmentation descriptors.
